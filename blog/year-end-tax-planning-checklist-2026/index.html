The year-end tax planning checklist for owner-operators (2026 edition)

Nino Mihilli· November 11, 2026 ·5 min read

Every January, some owner tells me they found out about a tax opportunity in March, after it was too late to use.

Most year-end tax moves only work if you make them before December 31. Once the year closes, your CPA is mostly documenting what already happened. November is the month to sit down with your numbers and your tax professional and decide on purpose.

This is general information, not tax advice. Tax rules depend on your entity, your state, and your specific facts. Use this list to prepare for the conversation with your CPA, not to replace it.

Step 1: Get a real year-to-date picture

You cannot plan taxes on a guess. Before you meet with your CPA, get:

  • A profit and loss statement through October, closed and reconciled
  • A balance sheet as of the same date
  • A projection for November and December, based on what is actually booked
  • Owner pay and distributions to date
  • A list of large purchases you are considering before year end

If your books are three months behind, fixing that is the first tax move. Planning on stale numbers produces stale advice.

Step 2: Check your estimated payments

If you expect to owe tax for the year, the final estimated payment for 2026 is due January 15, 2027, according to the IRS Taxpayer Advocate Service.

The IRS generally expects estimated payments if you expect to owe at least $1,000 after withholding and credits. Penalties can generally be avoided if your withholding and estimated payments cover the smaller of 90% of this year's tax or 100% of last year's tax — 110% of last year's if your prior-year adjusted gross income was over $150,000 ($75,000 if married filing separately).

A strong year can quietly put you behind. Ask your CPA to run the numbers now, while there is still time to adjust a payment instead of absorbing a penalty.

Step 3: Time equipment and asset purchases deliberately

The 2025 tax law changes made two depreciation rules much more generous, which makes timing decisions more important, not less.

  • Bonus depreciation: The IRS states that the law provides a permanent 100% additional first-year depreciation deduction for qualified property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025.
  • Section 179: For tax years beginning in 2026, the IRS sets the maximum Section 179 expense at $2,560,000, reduced dollar for dollar once qualifying purchases placed in service during the year exceed $4,090,000. Sport utility vehicles have a separate, lower cap of $32,000.

Two cautions I give every owner:

  1. "Placed in service" means in use, not ordered. Equipment sitting in a warehouse on December 31 may not count. Confirm the rule with your CPA.
  2. Never buy something you do not need to save tax. A deduction reduces tax on money you already spent. If the equipment does not earn its keep, a deduction does not rescue the decision.

There is also a quieter question: whether taking every deduction this year is the right move. If you expect higher income next year, your CPA may suggest a different approach. That is a judgment call that belongs with them.

Step 4: Get ready for the new 1099 threshold

This one changes the year-end routine for many businesses. According to the IRS, the reporting threshold for Form 1099-NEC rises from $600 to $2,000 for payments made after December 31, 2025, and may be adjusted for inflation beginning in 2027.

In practice, that means:

  • For payments made in 2026, contractors you paid less than $2,000 during the year generally do not need a 1099-NEC
  • Form 1099-NEC is due to recipients and to the IRS by January 31 (when that falls on a weekend, the next business day)
  • If you file 10 or more information returns in total, the IRS requires you to file electronically

Do not wait until January to collect W-9s. Pull your vendor list now, flag every contractor paid $2,000 or more, and request missing tax forms while people still answer email.

Step 5: Review owner compensation and distributions

If your business is an S corporation, the IRS requires reasonable compensation for shareholder-employees before non-wage distributions. Year end is the time to check whether the salary you paid yourself is defensible for the work you did. If you are a sole proprietor or partnership, review whether your draws and estimated payments line up with what you will actually owe.

Step 6: Look at retirement plan options before December 31

Retirement contributions can be one of the most useful year-end levers, but deadlines vary by plan type. Some plans must exist before year end for certain contributions to count. Ask your CPA and plan provider in November, not in April.

Step 7: Clean up the items that cause surprises

  • Receivables: Write off accounts you will truly never collect, and document why
  • Payables: Know which bills you will pay before year end and which after, and discuss the tax effect with your CPA if you use cash-basis accounting
  • Inventory: Schedule a count if you carry inventory
  • Payroll: Confirm year-end bonuses, fringe benefits, and any owner health insurance are handled correctly on W-2s
  • Records: Gather receipts for large expenses, vehicle logs, and home office documentation

What to bring to your CPA meeting

  1. Year-to-date P&L and balance sheet
  2. November and December projection
  3. List of planned major purchases with expected in-service dates
  4. Estimated payments made so far
  5. Owner compensation and distributions to date
  6. Contractor list with year-to-date totals
  7. Questions about retirement plans, entity structure, and next year's estimates

Why I treat this as stewardship

Taxes are not something to be clever about. They are something to be prepared for. Paying what you owe, on time, without surprises, is part of running a business responsibly. Planning ahead simply means you are not overpaying because you were too busy to look.
