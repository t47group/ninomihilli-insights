9 signs your business needs a fractional CFO

Nino Mihilli· October 14, 2026 ·5 min read

Most owners I talk to have a bookkeeper, a CPA, and a nagging feeling that something is missing.

The bookkeeper records what happened. The CPA files what happened, usually months after it happened. Nobody in the building is responsible for what is about to happen — the cash you will need in eleven weeks, the price increase you should have made last spring, the line of credit you should open before you need it.

That gap is the job of a CFO. And for most businesses between roughly half a million and twenty million in revenue, a full-time CFO is more than the company can carry. A fractional CFO fills the gap: senior financial judgment, a few days a month, at a fraction of the cost.

The question is when. Here are the signs I look for.

1. You find out about cash problems from your bank balance

If the first warning that payroll will be tight is logging into the bank account on Wednesday, you are managing cash by looking backward. A business at this size should see a shortfall weeks out, not days. If nobody is producing a rolling forecast, that is the clearest sign of all.

2. Your financials close late, or not at all

Monthly financials that arrive in the third week of the following month are history, not information. If you are routinely making decisions in October on August numbers, the decisions are guesses. A good finance function closes the books within ten business days and tells you what changed.

3. You know your revenue but not your margin by line

Plenty of owners can tell me top-line revenue to the dollar. Far fewer can tell me gross margin by customer, by service line, or by location. Growth hides margin problems. When you add revenue in the wrong place, you get busier and poorer at the same time.

4. You are about to borrow, raise, buy, or sell

Any transaction changes what you need from your numbers. A lender wants covenants you can meet. An investor wants a model that holds up. A buyer or seller needs a quality-of-earnings conversation long before the letter of intent. Walking into any of these with only tax-basis financials puts you at a disadvantage across the table.

5. Your CPA is doing strategy by default

Your CPA is valuable, and you should keep talking to them about tax and compliance. But if you are asking your tax preparer how to price a contract or whether to open a second location, you are asking someone to work outside their role. Most CPAs will tell you this themselves.

6. You cannot answer "what happens if we lose our biggest customer?"

Concentration risk is one of the most common hidden threats in a small business. If you cannot quickly model what losing your top customer does to cash, staffing, and debt service, you do not have a plan for it.

7. Growth is consuming cash instead of producing it

Fast-growing service businesses often run out of money while profitable on paper. Payroll goes out weekly or biweekly; customers pay in thirty, forty-five, or sixty days. Every new contract widens the gap. If you are growing and the bank balance keeps shrinking, you need someone managing working capital on purpose.

8. You make big decisions without a model

Hiring a sales team, signing a five-year lease, buying equipment, entering a new market. If these decisions get made on instinct and a quick look at the P&L, the downside is not that you will always be wrong. It is that when you are wrong, you will not see it until it is expensive.

9. You are the finance department

If you personally reconcile, forecast, chase receivables, negotiate with the bank, and build the budget, your time is being spent at the wrong altitude. Owners should be reviewing financial decisions, not producing every input.

What a fractional CFO should deliver

If you bring one in, hold them to concrete outputs. In the first ninety days, I would expect:

  • A clean, timely monthly close with a short written summary of what moved and why
  • A 13-week rolling cash flow forecast, updated weekly
  • Margin analysis by customer, service line, or location — whichever matters most in your business
  • A KPI dashboard of eight to twelve numbers you review every week
  • A financing plan — what credit you have, what you should have, and when to ask
  • An annual operating plan and budget, built with your managers, not handed down to them

If after ninety days you do not have most of those, the engagement is not working.

What it does not replace

A fractional CFO does not replace your CPA, your attorney, or your bookkeeper. Tax positions, entity structure, and legal questions still belong with licensed professionals. A good fractional CFO coordinates those people and makes sure they are working from the same numbers. They do not substitute for them.

It also does not replace you. The owner still makes the call. The difference is that you make it with a forecast, a margin picture, and someone willing to tell you the number you did not want to hear.

A quick self-assessment

Count how many of these are true today:

  1. I can see my cash position thirteen weeks from now.
  2. My monthly financials are closed within ten business days.
  3. I know gross margin by my main service lines or customers.
  4. I have a written budget and compare actuals to it monthly.
  5. I could model the loss of my largest customer in an afternoon.
  6. I have a banking relationship and credit line sized for my next stage.
  7. I review a short list of KPIs every week.

If you answered yes to five or more, you may be fine with your current setup plus periodic advice. If you answered yes to three or fewer, the cost of not having senior financial help is probably already higher than the cost of getting it.

The real reason to do it

The best argument for a fractional CFO is not better reports. It is fewer surprises. Surprises are what drain owners — the unexpected tax bill, the cash crunch in a record month, the contract that turned out to lose money on every hour.
