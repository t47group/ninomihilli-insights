Negotiating with integrity: how faith shapes the way I make deals

Nino Mihilli· November 6, 2026 ·5 min read

There is an old assumption in business that the best negotiator is the one who gives away the least and gets away with the most. Hide your position. Exploit the other side's weakness. Win the deal, whatever it takes.

I do not believe that, and over two decades of building businesses I have come to think it is not only wrong ethically but often wrong practically.

My guiding verse is Luke 6:31: "Do to others as you would have them do to you." It is a simple instruction, and it is hard to live out in a negotiation, where every instinct tells you to protect yourself first. But I have found it to be one of the most useful frameworks I have for how to conduct a deal.

Integrity in negotiation does not mean being soft. It means being clear, prepared, and fair, and being willing to walk away. Here is how that looks in practice.

Prepare more than the other side

Integrity starts with preparation. You cannot negotiate fairly if you do not understand what is fair.

Before any significant negotiation, I want to know:

  • My real objective. Not just the number, but what I need the deal to accomplish.
  • My walk-away point. The terms below which I will not proceed, decided before the conversation starts.
  • My alternatives. What I will do if this deal does not happen. Strong alternatives reduce the pressure to accept a bad deal.
  • Their interests. What the other party needs, what pressures they face, and what a good outcome looks like for them.
  • The market. What comparable deals look like, so I am anchored in reality.

Preparation protects both sides. It keeps me from overreaching out of ignorance and from conceding out of panic.

Tell the truth, and choose what to share

Integrity requires honesty. It does not require disclosing everything.

There is a meaningful difference between keeping your walk-away price to yourself and misrepresenting a fact. You are not obligated to tell the other side your maximum. You are obligated not to lie about what you know.

The lines I hold:

  • I do not make up competing offers or alternatives.
  • I do not misstate facts about a business, a product, or a situation.
  • I do not create false deadlines to pressure a decision.
  • When I know something material that the other side would reasonably need to know, I tell them, or I make sure the agreement accounts for it. Your attorney can advise on what disclosure is legally required in your situation.

Look for terms that serve both sides

Most negotiations are not purely about one number. They involve timing, payment terms, risk, scope, transition support, and many other variables.

When I understand what matters most to the other party, I can often give them something they value highly that costs me little, and ask for something I value highly that costs them little. That is how deals get better for both sides instead of just redistributing a fixed amount.

Questions that help:

  1. "What is most important to you in this agreement?"
  2. "What would make this a clear win on your side?"
  3. "What concerns you most about moving forward?"
  4. "If we could solve that, what else would need to be true?"

These are not tricks. They are a way of treating the other party as a person with real needs, which is what they are.

Do not exploit desperation

Sometimes you will negotiate with someone in a weak position: a seller under financial pressure, a vendor who needs the contract, an employee who has few other options.

It is legal to take advantage of that. It is usually not right, and it is rarely wise. People remember how they were treated when they were vulnerable. Markets are smaller than they look, especially in a city like Phoenix where business runs on relationships. A reputation for fair dealing compounds over a career.

This does not mean overpaying. It means offering a fair deal based on the actual value, not on the other side's lack of choices.

Keep your word after the signature

Integrity does not end at closing. Many deals fall apart in the execution: payment terms quietly stretched, commitments reinterpreted, promises from the negotiation forgotten.

Practical habits:

  • Put every material term in writing, and have your attorney review agreements before signing.
  • Send a short summary after key conversations so both sides share the same understanding.
  • Deliver what you promised, on time, without being chased.
  • When something changes, raise it early and directly.

Know when to walk away

The most important negotiating position is the willingness to leave. Walking away is not a failure of integrity. It is often an expression of it.

I walk away when:

  • The terms fall below my predetermined walk-away point
  • The other party is not being truthful
  • The deal would require me to compromise something I am not willing to compromise
  • The relationship would be unhealthy even if the terms look good

When you walk away, do it with respect. Thank them, be clear about why, and leave the door open if it makes sense. Sometimes the same deal comes back later on better terms.

An integrity checklist for your next negotiation

  • Have I written down my objective, walk-away point, and alternatives?
  • Do I understand what the other side needs?
  • Is everything I plan to say true?
  • Have I disclosed what fairness requires, with my attorney's input where needed?
  • Am I using any pressure tactic I would resent if it were used on me?
  • Would I be comfortable if this negotiation were described publicly?
  • Am I prepared to keep every promise I make?
  • Am I willing to walk away?

The long game

Integrity is not a strategy for winning a single negotiation. It is a strategy for building a business, and a life, that people trust. Over many years, that trust shows up in better partners, better deals, and relationships that last. It is a theme I return to often in my writing on faith and leadership.
