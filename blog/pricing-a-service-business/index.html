How to price a service business so growth doesn't kill your margin

Nino Mihilli· October 19, 2026 ·5 min read

There is a specific kind of exhaustion that comes from growing a service business with the wrong prices.

Revenue goes up. Headcount goes up. The office gets busier. And somehow the owner is taking home less than they were two years ago, working more hours, and lying awake about cash.

Usually the cause is not effort or demand. It is pricing that was set once, early, and never rebuilt as the business changed. Service businesses sell time and expertise, and when the price of that time is wrong, every new customer makes the problem bigger.

Here is how I approach pricing in a service business.

Start with your fully loaded cost per hour

Most owners know what they pay an employee per hour. Very few know what that hour actually costs the business.

The fully loaded cost includes:

  • Wages
  • Employer payroll taxes
  • Workers' compensation and other required insurance
  • Benefits
  • Paid time off, holidays, and training time
  • A share of supervision and management
  • A share of overhead: rent, software, vehicles, equipment, admin staff

Then there is the part people miss: utilization. An employee paid for 2,080 hours a year does not bill 2,080 hours. Between time off, training, travel, admin, and gaps between jobs, the billable portion is often much lower. Your cost has to be spread across the hours you can actually bill, not the hours you pay for.

The calculation is simple:

Fully loaded annual cost of a role ÷ realistic billable hours per year = true cost per billable hour

Do this for each major role. The number is almost always higher than owners expect.

Set a margin floor before you set a price

Once you know cost, decide the minimum gross margin you will accept on any piece of work. Write it down. Different industries run very different margins, so the right floor depends on your business — your CPA or a peer group in your industry can help you sanity-check it.

The point of the floor is discipline. When a large customer asks for a discount, the conversation stops being "can we afford to lose them?" and becomes "does this still clear our floor?" If it does not, you are being asked to pay for the privilege of serving them.

Price the value, then check the cost

Cost-plus pricing protects you from losing money. It does not tell you what the work is worth.

Ask what your service changes for the customer. Does it prevent downtime, reduce turnover, save their managers time, reduce risk? A customer who avoids a costly problem because of your work is not comparing your rate to your cost. They are comparing it to the problem.

My working approach:

  1. Set the price based on value and the market
  2. Check it against your fully loaded cost and margin floor
  3. If value-based pricing does not clear the floor, change the scope or delivery model — not the floor

Where growth quietly erodes margin

Watch these as you grow:

  • Legacy pricing. Your first customers often have your oldest, lowest rates, and they tend to be the largest accounts by now.
  • Scope creep. Small "while you're here" requests that are never billed add up across hundreds of jobs.
  • Volume discounts that never got re-earned. A discount given for a volume commitment should end if the volume does.
  • Rising labor costs without rising prices. Wages, insurance, and benefits move every year. If your prices do not, your margin shrinks automatically.
  • Unbillable growth. New management layers, systems, and admin roles are necessary, but someone has to fund them, and that is your pricing.

How to raise prices without losing good customers

Many owners delay price increases because they fear losing customers. In my experience, most reasonable increases, explained clearly and delivered with good notice, are accepted by the customers you most want to keep.

A practical sequence:

  1. Segment your customers by margin, not revenue. Identify the accounts below your floor.
  2. Start with new customers. New pricing applies to all new work immediately.
  3. Give existing customers notice — thirty to sixty days is common — in writing.
  4. Explain the why in plain language: labor, insurance, and service investments.
  5. Offer a choice where it makes sense: same price for adjusted scope, or same scope at the new price.
  6. Build an annual review into your contracts so future adjustments are expected, not negotiated from scratch. Have your attorney review contract language.
  7. Be willing to let a few go. A customer who only stays at a price below your floor is not a customer you can afford to grow with.

Treat customers the way you would want to be treated: no surprise invoices, no hidden fees, clear notice. That is Luke 6:31 applied to pricing, and it keeps the relationship intact even when the number goes up.

A pricing review checklist

Run this at least once a year:

  • Fully loaded cost per billable hour calculated for each major role
  • Realistic utilization measured, not assumed
  • Written margin floor set and shared with anyone who quotes work
  • Gross margin calculated by customer for your top twenty accounts
  • Every account below the floor flagged with a plan
  • Discounts listed and tied to a reason that still applies
  • Contracts include a price review clause
  • Out-of-scope work has a written rate and a process to bill it

Pricing is a leadership decision

Pricing is often treated as a sales task. It is not. It determines whether you can pay your people well, invest in better systems, and build a business that lasts. In staffing, at FLAT Staffing, and across the companies I work with, the margin conversation always comes back to the same truth: you cannot serve customers well for long at a price that does not sustain the team doing the work.
