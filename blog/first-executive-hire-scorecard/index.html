Hiring your first executive: a role scorecard and 90-day plan

Nino Mihilli· October 23, 2026 ·5 min read

The first executive hire is one of the most expensive decisions a growing company makes, and one of the least structured.

Owners who would never buy a piece of equipment without specs will hire a general manager, operations director, or VP of sales based on a few conversations and a good feeling. When it works, it changes the business. When it does not, the cost is a year of lost momentum, a disrupted team, and an owner who concludes that "nobody can do it like I do."

Usually the problem is not the candidate. It is that the role was never defined well enough for anyone to succeed in it.

This is the process I recommend, built around two documents: a scorecard before you hire and a 90-day plan after.

Step 1: Decide what you are actually handing off

Before writing a job description, write down what comes off your desk. Be specific. Not "operations," but:

  • Weekly scheduling and staffing decisions
  • Vendor selection and negotiation under a set dollar amount
  • Hiring and firing of frontline supervisors
  • The weekly operations meeting
  • Customer escalations below a certain account size

Then write what stays with you. Clarity on both sides prevents the most common failure: an executive hired to lead who is never actually allowed to.

Step 2: Write the scorecard

A scorecard is a one-page document that defines success in the role. It has three parts.

Mission. One or two sentences on why the role exists. For example: "Run day-to-day operations so that service quality improves while the owner shifts focus to sales and strategy."

Outcomes. Three to five measurable results expected within the first twelve to eighteen months. Each should be something you could verify, such as:

  • On-time delivery rate reaches a defined target
  • Gross margin improves by a defined amount
  • Two supervisor roles filled and retained through year one
  • Weekly KPI review running without the owner leading it

Competencies. Five to eight behaviors the person must demonstrate. Pick ones that matter for this role in this company. A turnaround needs different strengths than a steady-state operation.

The scorecard becomes the basis for the job posting, the interview questions, the reference checks, and the first performance review. Everything ties back to it.

Step 3: Structure the interviews

Unstructured interviews mostly measure how much you like someone. A structured process measures whether they can do the job.

A sequence that works for most small and mid-size companies:

  1. Screening call (30 minutes). Confirm basics: compensation range, location, availability, and whether their experience fits the scale of your business.
  2. Career walk-through (90 minutes). Go through each prior role in order. For each, ask what they were hired to do, what they accomplished, what went poorly, who they worked with, and why they left. Patterns across roles tell you more than any single answer.
  3. Outcome interviews (60 minutes each). For each outcome on your scorecard, ask how they have achieved something similar. Push for specifics: numbers, timelines, their role versus the team's role.
  4. Working session. Give them a real problem from your business and work through it together. You will learn how they think, how they ask questions, and how they handle a gap in information.
  5. Team conversation. Have two or three people who would report to them spend time with the finalist.

Write down your assessment against the scorecard after every interview, before discussing with anyone else.

Step 4: Check references properly

Reference checks are often a formality. They should not be.

Ask the candidate to arrange calls with former bosses, peers, and people who reported to them. Then ask each reference:

  • What were this person's biggest strengths?
  • What were their biggest areas for improvement at the time?
  • How would you rate their overall performance, one to ten? What would have made it higher?
  • Would you hire them again for a similar role?

The scoring question matters. Vague praise is common. A specific number with a specific reason is useful.

Background and credential checks should follow your attorney's guidance and applicable employment law.

Step 5: Build the 90-day plan before day one

The first ninety days decide most executive hires. Write the plan before they start and review it together in the first week.

Days 1–30: Learn

  • Meet every direct report and key customer
  • Sit in on every recurring meeting without changing it yet
  • Review the financials, KPIs, and current problems
  • Deliver a written summary of what they see: strengths, risks, and early questions

Days 31–60: Prioritize

  • Agree with the owner on the two or three priorities for the next six months
  • Take ownership of the meetings and decisions in their area
  • Make one or two visible improvements that build credibility with the team

Days 61–90: Own

  • Run their area with the owner in an advisory role
  • Report against scorecard outcomes in the weekly review
  • Present a six-month plan with milestones

Step 6: Get out of the way, on purpose

This is the step most owners struggle with. If you hired well, the new executive will make decisions differently than you would. Some will be better. Some will be worse. A few will be mistakes.

Agree on how you will handle disagreement. A rule I like: the executive decides within their area, and the owner can raise concerns privately but does not overrule in front of the team, except for matters of ethics, safety, or serious financial risk.

Schedule a weekly one-on-one and protect it. That is where coaching happens, so it does not leak into undermining.

A hiring readiness checklist

Before you post the role, confirm:

  • Handoff list written: what leaves your desk and what stays
  • Scorecard complete with mission, outcomes, and competencies
  • Compensation range set and checked against the market
  • Budget confirmed for at least eighteen months of the role
  • Interview sequence and interviewers scheduled
  • Reference questions prepared
  • 90-day plan drafted
  • Your own weekly calendar adjusted to make room for the transition

Why this matters

Bringing in an executive is an act of trust, and the person you hire is trusting you with their career. Treating them the way you would want to be treated means giving them a clear job, the authority to do it, and an honest measure of how they are doing.
