One of the most common questions I hear from owners who run an S corporation is some version of this: "How little salary can I pay myself?"

I understand the logic. In an S corporation, wages carry payroll taxes and distributions generally do not. So the temptation is to pay a small salary and take the rest as distributions.

The problem is that the IRS has a clear position on this, and the question is the wrong one. The better question is: what is a reasonable salary for the work I actually do, and can I defend it?

This is general information, not tax advice. S corporation compensation is fact-specific. Work with a CPA who understands your business before you set or change your pay.

## What the IRS says

According to the IRS, an S corporation must pay reasonable compensation to a shareholder-employee for services provided to the corporation before taking non-wage distributions. The IRS also states that, generally, an officer of a corporation is an employee of the corporation, and that payments for an officer's services should be treated as wages.

The IRS can reclassify distributions as wages when it believes salary was unreasonably low. That can mean back payroll taxes, penalties, and interest.

The IRS frames a key issue this way: where do the company's gross receipts come from? Receipts generated by the owner's personal services point toward wages. Receipts generated by non-shareholder employees, capital, or equipment can support distributions.

## The factors that matter

The IRS lists factors used in determining reasonable compensation, including:

- Training and experience
- Duties and responsibilities
- Time and effort devoted to the business
- Dividend history
- Payments to non-shareholder employees
- Timing and manner of paying bonuses to key people
- What comparable businesses pay for similar services
- Compensation agreements
- Use of a formula to determine compensation

Notice that none of these is "what minimizes my tax." Each one asks what the work is worth.

## A practical way to think about it

Here is how I would approach the conversation with your CPA.

**1. List the roles you actually perform.** Many owners are the CEO, the head of sales, the operations manager, and sometimes the bookkeeper. Write down each role and roughly how much of your time it takes.

**2. Ask what you would pay someone else.** If you hired a person to do the work you do, what would the market require? Your CPA may use compensation data or a formal study to support this.

**3. Separate the return on your investment from the pay for your work.** If the business earns profit because of employees, systems, equipment, and capital you have invested, that portion can reasonably flow as distributions. If the profit exists mostly because of your own hours, more of it looks like wages.

**4. Consider the business's ability to pay.** A reasonable salary should still leave the company able to meet its obligations. If the business cannot support a market salary, document why.

**5. Write it down.** Keep a short memo each year explaining how the salary was set, what data was used, and what changed. Documentation will not make an unreasonable number reasonable, but it shows the decision was made thoughtfully.

## Health insurance for owners

Owners holding more than 2% of an S corporation have a specific rule for health insurance. According to the IRS, premiums paid by the S corporation for a greater-than-2% shareholder-employee are deductible by the corporation and reported as wages on the shareholder's Form W-2. When provided under a plan covering employees generally, those amounts are subject to income tax withholding but not Social Security, Medicare, or FUTA taxes.

The IRS also notes that the shareholder may be able to take a deduction for those premiums on their personal return if the requirements are met, and that the premiums must ultimately be paid by the S corporation — directly or by reimbursement — and reported on the W-2.

This is a common year-end payroll miss. If the company pays your health insurance, make sure your payroll provider and CPA know before W-2s are prepared.

## Mistakes I see

- **Zero salary with large distributions.** If you work in the business full time and take no wages, expect questions.
- **Setting salary once and never revisiting it.** As the business grows, your role and the market rate change.
- **Taking distributions irregularly to cover personal bills.** Unpredictable distributions make cash planning harder and can blur the line between wages and distributions.
- **Ignoring distributions in cash planning.** Distributions are real cash leaving the business. They belong in your 13-week forecast.
- **Uneven distributions among shareholders.** The IRS requires an S corporation to have only one class of stock, and distributions that do not track ownership can raise questions about that status. Talk to your CPA and attorney before paying owners differently.

## A year-end owner pay checklist

1. Total salary paid year to date
2. Total distributions year to date
3. Written description of your roles and time
4. Market comparison for those roles
5. Health insurance premiums paid by the company, reported correctly
6. Planned December payroll and any bonus
7. Notes for next year's salary decision

## Stewardship applies to the owner, too

Paying yourself fairly is not selfish, and paying yourself carefully is not greedy. Both are part of running the business honestly. A reasonable salary keeps your tax position defensible, gives you a clearer picture of what the business really earns after paying for the owner's work, and sets an honest standard if you ever bring in a partner, a buyer, or a successor.
