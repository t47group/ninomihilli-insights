Every owner I know is busy. Not every owner knows what is happening in their business.

Those are different things. You can spend sixty hours a week inside a company and still be surprised by a cash crunch, a customer leaving, or a team that quietly fell behind three weeks ago. Activity is not visibility.

The fix I recommend is simple and unglamorous: a thirty-minute review of the same ten or so numbers every Monday morning. Same numbers, same order, same time. Over months it becomes the heartbeat of the business.

## Why weekly, and why Monday

Monthly financials are essential, but they are slow. By the time the books close, you are reviewing results that are four to seven weeks old. A weekly review catches problems while they are still small.

Monday works because it sets the week. You see where you stand, decide what to fix, and have five days to do it. Some owners prefer Friday afternoon. The day matters less than the consistency.

## The ten numbers

Your exact list depends on your business, but most owner-operated companies need something from each of these categories.

**Cash**

1. **Cash on hand** — actual bank balance, all operating accounts
2. **13-week cash low point** — the lowest projected balance in your rolling forecast, and which week it lands

**Sales**

3. **New sales or bookings this week** — signed work, not proposals
4. **Pipeline value** — weighted or unweighted, as long as it is consistent
5. **Leads or qualified opportunities** — the front of the funnel; it predicts revenue months out

**Delivery**

6. **Capacity utilization** — billable hours, filled shifts, jobs completed, units shipped, whatever represents productive output
7. **Quality or service measure** — on-time completion, rework, complaints, customer satisfaction score

**Money in and out**

8. **Accounts receivable over 60 days** — the dollar amount, and the top three accounts in it
9. **Gross margin, trailing four weeks** — if you can calculate it weekly; if not, a proxy like labor cost as a percentage of revenue

**People**

10. **Headcount and open roles** — plus any turnover that week

That is enough. If your dashboard has thirty metrics, you will stop reading it. If it has three, you will miss things.

## Set a target and a trigger for each

A number with no reference point is just trivia. For each KPI, write down two things:

- **Target:** where you want it to be
- **Trigger:** the level at which someone must act, without waiting for permission

For example, if receivables over sixty days cross a set amount, the trigger might be that the owner personally calls the top three accounts that week. Triggers turn a report into a management system.

Color coding helps: green at or above target, yellow between target and trigger, red past the trigger. You should be able to scan the sheet in under a minute and know where to look.

## How to run the meeting

Keep it to thirty minutes. Here is an agenda that works:

1. **Scorecard review (10 minutes).** Go down the list. Green items get no discussion. Yellow and red items get a one-sentence explanation from the owner of that number.
2. **Issues (15 minutes).** Pick the two or three most important red items. Discuss the cause, not just the symptom. Decide on an action.
3. **Commitments (5 minutes).** Each action gets one owner and a due date. Write them down.

Next Monday, start by reviewing last week's commitments. Done or not done. No long stories.

## Who owns the numbers

Every KPI needs one person who is responsible for reporting it and explaining it. Not the person who is to blame when it is red — the person who understands it best and can drive it.

In a small company, the owner may hold several. As you grow, push ownership down to managers. When a sales manager owns pipeline and an operations lead owns utilization, the meeting becomes a conversation among leaders instead of the owner interrogating the room.

## Building your scorecard in a week

1. **Monday:** List every number you currently look at, formally or informally.
2. **Tuesday:** Cut the list to ten to twelve, using the categories above.
3. **Wednesday:** Identify the source for each number and who will report it.
4. **Thursday:** Set a target and trigger for each. Use recent history as a starting point.
5. **Friday:** Build a simple spreadsheet with weeks across the top and KPIs down the side.
6. **Next Monday:** Run your first review. Expect it to be messy.

After four to six weeks, revisit the list. Drop any number that never drives a decision. Add anything that surprised you.

## Common traps

- **Waiting for perfect data.** A rough number every week beats a precise number every quarter.
- **Too many vanity metrics.** Website visits and social followers rarely belong on an owner's weekly list unless they directly predict revenue.
- **No history.** Keep every week in the same sheet. Trends matter more than any single week.
- **Skipping weeks.** The value comes from rhythm. A review that happens "when we have time" does not happen.
- **Discussing green numbers.** Celebrate them briefly and move on. The meeting exists to fix things.

## The point of all this

A weekly KPI review is not about control. It is about giving your leaders a shared, honest picture of reality so they can make better decisions without waiting on you. It also builds trust: when everyone sees the same numbers, conversations get shorter and fairer.
