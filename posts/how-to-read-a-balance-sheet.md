Most owners read the profit and loss statement. Far fewer read the balance sheet.

That is understandable. The P&L answers the question everyone cares about: did we make money? The balance sheet looks like a list of accounts with no story attached. But the balance sheet is where many of the problems the P&L hides show up first — cash being tied up in receivables, debt creeping up, taxes owed but not set aside, an owner taking out more than the business earned.

If you learn to read it, you will see trouble months earlier. Here is how I approach it.

## What the balance sheet is

The P&L covers a period of time: a month, a quarter, a year. The balance sheet is a snapshot of a single date. It shows what the business owns, what it owes, and what is left over for the owners on that day.

It always follows one equation:

> Assets = Liabilities + Equity

If the two sides do not match, something is wrong with the books.

## Section 1: Assets — what the business owns

Assets are usually split into two groups.

**Current assets** are expected to turn into cash within about a year:

- **Cash** in operating, payroll, and savings accounts
- **Accounts receivable** — money customers owe you
- **Inventory**, if you carry it
- **Prepaid expenses**, such as insurance paid in advance

**Long-term assets** are used over several years:

- Vehicles, equipment, furniture, and computers, shown net of accumulated depreciation
- Buildings or leasehold improvements
- Deposits and other long-term items

## Section 2: Liabilities — what the business owes

**Current liabilities** are due within about a year:

- **Accounts payable** — bills owed to vendors
- **Credit cards**
- **Accrued payroll and payroll taxes** — wages and taxes earned but not yet paid
- **Sales tax payable**, if you collect it
- **Line of credit balances**
- **The current portion of long-term debt** — principal due in the next twelve months

**Long-term liabilities** are due beyond a year, such as the remaining balance on term loans or equipment financing.

## Section 3: Equity — what belongs to the owners

Equity is what remains after subtracting liabilities from assets. It usually includes money the owners put in, profits kept in the business over time, and reductions for distributions or draws taken out.

Equity is not cash. A business can show strong equity and still have very little money in the bank, because the value is tied up in receivables, inventory, or equipment.

## The lines I check every month

**1. Cash compared with last month.** Then ask why it moved. If the P&L shows a profit and cash went down, the answer is somewhere else on the balance sheet.

**2. Accounts receivable compared with revenue.** If receivables grow faster than sales, customers are paying more slowly, or invoices are stuck. Pair this with your aging report.

**3. Accounts payable and credit cards.** Rising payables can mean the business is quietly financing itself by paying vendors late. That works until a key vendor puts you on hold.

**4. Payroll and sales tax liabilities.** These should be small and current. Money withheld from employees or collected from customers is not the business's money. A growing balance here deserves immediate attention. Confirm deposit and filing rules with your CPA and payroll provider.

**5. Line of credit balance.** A line should go up and come back down. If it only goes up, it is funding something the business is not covering on its own.

**6. Total debt.** Add up every loan, line, and credit card. Watch the trend over several months, not just one.

**7. Owner draws or distributions.** Compare what owners took out with what the business earned. If distributions consistently exceed profit, equity shrinks and the business gets weaker.

**8. Anything labeled "suspense," "uncategorized," or "ask my accountant."** These are clean-up items. A large or growing balance means your books are not ready to make decisions on.

## Three simple ratios

You do not need a finance degree for these. They are just quick ways to see whether things are getting better or worse.

**Current ratio**

> Current assets ÷ Current liabilities

This shows whether short-term assets cover short-term obligations. A falling ratio means the cushion is getting thinner.

**Working capital**

> Current assets - Current liabilities

This is the dollar version of the same idea. Growth usually consumes working capital, because you carry more receivables and payroll before customers pay.

**Debt to equity**

> Total liabilities ÷ Total equity

This shows how much of the business is funded by others compared with the owners. A rising number means more risk if revenue drops.

What counts as healthy varies widely by industry and business model, so the trend matters more than a single reading. Your CPA, banker, or a peer group in your industry can help you understand what is normal for a business like yours. Lenders often look at these numbers too, and some loan agreements include covenants based on them.

## Common balance sheet problems

- **Bank accounts that are not reconciled.** If the cash line does not match the bank, nothing else can be trusted.
- **Old receivables that will never be collected.** They make assets look bigger than they are. Review them with your CPA.
- **Loan balances that do not match the lender's statement.** This usually means payments were recorded incorrectly.
- **Personal expenses mixed into the business.** They distort both the P&L and equity.
- **Equipment that was sold or scrapped but still listed.**

## A monthly balance sheet routine

1. Confirm all bank, credit card, and loan accounts are reconciled
2. Compare each major line with last month and the same month last year
3. Review the eight lines above
4. Calculate the three ratios and note the trend
5. Write down two or three questions for your bookkeeper or CPA
6. Follow up before the next month closes

Once the books are clean, this takes about twenty minutes.

## Knowing what you are responsible for

The balance sheet is, in a real sense, a statement of what has been entrusted to you: the money customers owe, the obligations to vendors and lenders, the taxes held on behalf of employees and the government. Reading it every month is part of being a faithful steward of all of it. It is one of the habits I encourage most often when I work with owners on financial strategy.
