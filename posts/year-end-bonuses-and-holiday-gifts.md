December is when many owners decide to say thank you to their team. A bonus, a gift card, a holiday party, a turkey for every household.

Those are good instincts. Gratitude is one of the most underrated tools a leader has. But year-end rewards also run through payroll and the tax code, and a generous gesture handled carelessly can turn into a correction in January, a surprise on someone's W-2, or a bonus that feels smaller than promised.

Here is how I would plan year-end rewards so they land the way you intend.

This is general information, not tax or legal advice. Confirm the treatment of any bonus or benefit with your CPA and payroll provider.

## Decide what you are rewarding

Before the dollars, decide the purpose. A year-end payment can mean very different things:

- **A thank-you to everyone** for the year, the same or similar for each person
- **A performance bonus** tied to specific results
- **A retention bonus** meant to keep key people through a transition
- **Profit sharing** tied to how the business did

Mixing these up causes resentment. If the bonus is discretionary, say so. If it is tied to performance, explain the criteria. If it is a thank-you, do not attach conditions to it after the fact.

And be careful with promises. Talking about a bonus all year and then not paying it, or calling something "guaranteed," can create obligations. Have your attorney review written bonus plans.

## How cash bonuses are taxed

Bonuses are wages. They run through payroll like any other pay, with income tax withholding and payroll taxes.

The IRS treats bonuses as supplemental wages. According to IRS Publication 15, the federal withholding rate on supplemental wages remains 22% for 2026, and 37% on supplemental wages above $1 million paid to an employee in a calendar year. Your payroll provider may use that flat rate or combine the bonus with regular wages, depending on how it is paid. State withholding rules vary.

Social Security and Medicare taxes also apply. For 2026, the IRS lists the Social Security tax rate at 6.2% each for employer and employee on wages up to $184,500, and Medicare at 1.45% each with no wage base limit.

Two practical consequences:

1. **The employee takes home less than the gross amount.** If you want someone to receive a specific net amount, ask your payroll provider to "gross up" the bonus. That costs the business more.
2. **The business pays its share of payroll taxes.** Budget for it.

Never pay a bonus outside payroll as a check from the business account with no withholding. It is still wages.

## Gift cards are not gifts, tax-wise

This is the mistake I see most often. An owner hands out gift cards at the holiday party and assumes they are a tax-free gift.

They are not. The IRS states that cash and cash-equivalent fringe benefits — including gift cards and gift certificates — are never excludable as de minimis benefits, no matter how small. That means a gift card is treated as taxable wages and should go through payroll.

If you want to give gift cards, that is fine. Just tell your payroll provider so the value is included correctly.

## What can be a non-taxable holiday gift

The IRS allows certain de minimis fringe benefits — small, occasional benefits where accounting for them would be unreasonable — to be excluded from wages. IRS Publication 15-B lists examples including:

- Holiday or birthday gifts, other than cash, with a low fair market value
- Occasional parties or picnics for employees and their guests
- Occasional snacks and coffee

The IRS does not set a single dollar limit for de minimis benefits. Value and frequency both matter, and a benefit that is too large is fully taxable. A modest holiday gift item for each employee is a common example. An expensive electronic device probably is not. Check with your CPA if you are unsure.

## Gifts to clients and partners

Many owners also send gifts to customers and referral partners at year end. The tax rules are different.

According to IRS Publication 463, you can deduct no more than $25 for business gifts given to each person during your tax year. Incidental costs such as engraving, packaging, insuring, and mailing generally do not count toward that limit. And an item costing $4 or less with your business name permanently imprinted, distributed widely, is not treated as a gift for this purpose.

That does not mean you cannot spend more. It means the deduction is limited. Give for the relationship, not the write-off.

Also check whether your customers have gift policies. Many larger companies limit what their employees can accept, and the kindest gift is one that does not put your contact in an awkward spot.

## Planning the timing

A few timing questions to raise with your CPA:

- **Which tax year should the bonus fall in?** Paying in December versus January can change which year the business deducts it and which year the employee reports it.
- **Is your payroll calendar ready?** Confirm processing deadlines with your provider. Year-end and holiday schedules move fast.
- **Will the bonus change benefit calculations?** Some retirement plans count bonuses as compensation for contribution purposes. Check your plan document.

## A year-end rewards checklist

1. Decide the purpose of each payment: thank-you, performance, retention, or profit share
2. Set a total budget that includes the employer's share of payroll taxes
3. Confirm all cash bonuses and gift cards run through payroll
4. Decide whether to gross up any bonuses
5. Keep non-cash employee gifts modest and occasional
6. Track client gifts by recipient for the $25 deduction rule
7. Confirm payroll deadlines for December
8. Communicate clearly and personally

## Gratitude done well

The money matters, but how you say thank you matters as much. A short, specific note — what this person did this year that made a difference — often means more than the number on the check. People tend to remember being seen long after they forget the amount.
