Right now, many of your best customers are building their budgets and plans for next year.

They are deciding which vendors to keep, which to consolidate, what to bid out, and what to spend more on. If you are not part of that conversation, you will find out what they decided when it is already done.

That is why I believe every owner should hold a year-end review with their most important customers, ideally before the holidays slow everything down. Not a sales pitch. A structured, honest conversation about how the year went and what next year needs to look like.

The week of Thanksgiving is a good time to think about this. Gratitude for the customers who trusted you this year is the right starting point for the conversation.

## Who gets a review

You cannot do this with everyone, so be deliberate. Start with:

- Your top ten customers by gross profit, not just revenue
- Any customer where the relationship has felt strained this year
- Customers with contracts up for renewal in the next six months
- Customers who have grown quickly and may need a different level of service

For smaller accounts, a short written note and a quick call can serve the same purpose.

## Prepare before you meet

A good review starts with homework. For each customer, gather:

1. **The year in numbers:** volume, spend, service levels, and any measures you report on
2. **What went well:** specific wins, projects, or problems you helped solve
3. **What went wrong:** missed deadlines, quality issues, billing errors, complaints — and what you did about them
4. **What you know about their next year:** expansion, new locations, budget pressure, leadership changes
5. **Your own view of the account:** margin, payment history, and whether the relationship is healthy for both sides

If something went wrong this year, do not wait for them to bring it up. Owning a mistake before the customer mentions it does more for trust than almost anything else you can say.

## Who should be in the room

On your side, the owner or a senior leader, plus the person who manages the account day to day. On their side, ask for your main contact and, if possible, their manager or the person who controls the budget.

Keep it to an hour. Offer to come to them.

## A simple agenda

**1. Thank them (5 minutes).** Specifically. Name something they did that made the work better.

**2. Review the year (15 minutes).** Brief. Share the numbers and highlights. Acknowledge what went wrong and what you changed.

**3. Listen (25 minutes).** This is the core of the meeting. Ask open questions and let them talk.

**4. Look ahead (10 minutes).** Discuss their plans and how you can support them.

**5. Agree on next steps (5 minutes).** Two or three specific commitments with dates.

## Questions that open real conversation

- "What is the most important thing we did for you this year?"
- "Where did we make your job harder?"
- "If you could change one thing about how we work together, what would it be?"
- "What is changing in your business next year?"
- "What are you under the most pressure to deliver?"
- "Are there other areas of your business where you are struggling with something we might help with?"
- "How do you evaluate vendors at renewal time, and how are we doing against that?"
- "Is there anyone else in your organization we should be talking to?"

Then be quiet. The most valuable thing a customer says usually comes after a pause.

## What not to do

- **Do not pitch.** If a real opportunity comes up, schedule a separate conversation.
- **Do not get defensive.** If they raise a complaint, thank them, ask questions, and commit to a response.
- **Do not bring up a price increase as a surprise.** If one is coming, it deserves its own clear, advance conversation in writing.
- **Do not skip the follow-up.** A review with no follow-up is worse than no review, because it shows you were not really listening.

## Turn what you hear into a plan

Within a week of each meeting, send a short written summary:

- What you heard
- What you are committing to do
- Dates for each commitment
- When you will check in next

Then bring what you learned back to your own team. Look for patterns across customers. If three of your top accounts mention the same frustration, that is not a customer problem. It is an operating problem, and it belongs in your annual plan.

I like to capture the results in a simple grid for each top account:

- **Health:** strong, stable, or at risk
- **Growth potential next year:** high, medium, or low
- **Key commitments:** what we promised
- **Owner on our side:** who is responsible

That grid becomes part of how you prioritize sales, service, and staffing for the new year.

## The quiet benefit

Customers rarely leave over a single mistake. They leave when they feel ignored, when problems pile up without anyone acknowledging them, or when a competitor pays more attention than you do. A year-end review is a simple way to show that you are paying attention.

It also reflects how I believe business relationships should work. Luke 6:31 — treat others the way you want to be treated — applies to customers as much as anyone. Most of us would want our vendors to sit down with us once a year, ask how they are really doing, and act on the answer.
