Co-owners almost always start out aligned. They share a vision, trust each other, and cannot imagine a day when they would disagree about something fundamental.

Then life happens. A partner wants out. Someone gets sick. A divorce puts part of the company in play. A partner dies, and their spouse suddenly owns a share of a business they have never worked in. Without a plan, each of those situations can turn into a dispute about price, control, and money, at the worst possible moment.

A buy-sell agreement is how co-owners decide those questions in advance, while everyone is still on good terms. If your business has more than one owner and you do not have one, it belongs near the top of your list for 2027.

This is general information, not legal or tax advice. Buy-sell agreements have serious legal, tax, and estate consequences. Work with a business attorney, a CPA, and, where relevant, an insurance professional and a valuation expert.

## What a buy-sell agreement does

At its core, a buy-sell agreement answers four questions:

1. **What events trigger a buyout?**
2. **Who buys the departing owner's interest** — the other owners, the company, or either?
3. **At what price?**
4. **How will the buyout be paid for?**

It may be a standalone agreement or part of your operating agreement or shareholder agreement. What matters is that it exists, it is current, and every owner understands it.

## The triggers to cover

Common triggering events include:

- **Death** of an owner
- **Disability** that prevents an owner from working
- **Retirement** or voluntary departure
- **Termination** of an owner who is also an employee
- **Divorce**, which can otherwise put ownership in the hands of an ex-spouse
- **Bankruptcy** or creditor claims against an owner
- **Deadlock** between owners who cannot agree on major decisions
- **A desire to sell** to an outside party

For each trigger, the agreement should say whether a buyout is required or optional, and on what terms. A departing owner who leaves voluntarily after two years may be treated differently from the family of an owner who dies after twenty.

## Setting the price

This is where most agreements succeed or fail. Common approaches:

- **Fixed price,** agreed by the owners and updated periodically. Simple, but often goes stale when owners forget to update it.
- **Formula,** such as a multiple of earnings or book value. Predictable, but may not reflect real value as the business changes.
- **Independent appraisal** at the time of the trigger. Usually more accurate, but takes time and cost, and the agreement must spell out how appraisers are chosen and what happens if valuations differ.

Whichever method you choose, define the details: the valuation date, whether discounts apply, how debt is treated, and how disputes are resolved. Vague price language is an invitation to conflict.

## Funding the buyout

A buyout price is meaningless if nobody can pay it. Common funding approaches:

- **Life and disability insurance,** owned by the company or by the other owners, to provide cash when an owner dies or becomes disabled
- **Installment payments** over several years, often with interest and security
- **Company cash reserves** set aside over time
- **Outside financing,** which depends on the business's ability to borrow at that moment

Many agreements combine methods: insurance for death and disability, installments for retirement or voluntary departure.

## How the structure affects taxes

The structure of the agreement — whether the company buys the interest or the remaining owners do, and who owns any insurance — can have very different tax results.

A clear example came from the U.S. Supreme Court in *Connelly v. United States*, decided June 6, 2024. The Court held that a corporation's contractual obligation to redeem shares is not necessarily a liability that reduces the corporation's value for federal estate tax purposes. In that case, life insurance proceeds the company received to fund a redemption counted toward the company's value, which increased the value of the deceased owner's shares for estate tax purposes.

If your agreement uses company-owned life insurance to fund a redemption, ask your attorney and CPA whether the structure still fits your goals. This is exactly the kind of question that should be reviewed, not assumed.

## Other terms worth including

- **Transfer restrictions** that prevent owners from selling or pledging their interests without the others' consent
- **Right of first refusal** if an owner receives an outside offer
- **Non-compete and non-solicitation** terms for departing owners, subject to state law
- **Spousal consent** so spouses acknowledge and agree to the terms
- **Dispute resolution** procedures, such as mediation before litigation
- **A review schedule** so the agreement stays current

## When to review it

A buy-sell agreement is not a one-time document. Review it:

- Every year or two, especially if it uses a fixed price
- When the business's value changes significantly
- When an owner joins or leaves
- When an owner's family circumstances change
- When tax law or court decisions affect your structure
- When insurance policies are renewed or changed

Year end is a natural time to put it on the agenda. Many owners already meet with their CPA and attorney in this season.

## A buy-sell checklist

1. Confirm whether you have an agreement and where it is
2. Read it with your co-owners, together
3. List the triggers it covers and the ones it misses
4. Check whether the valuation method still makes sense
5. Confirm how each trigger would be funded
6. Review insurance ownership and amounts with your advisors
7. Ask your attorney and CPA to review structure and tax effects
8. Set a date for the next review

## A gift to your partners and your families

A buy-sell agreement can feel like planning for a breakup. I see it as the opposite. It protects the relationship by settling hard questions before emotions and money are on the line. It protects your partner's family, and yours, from being forced into a business they never chose. And it protects employees and customers from the disruption of an ownership fight. Agreeing on fair terms in advance is Luke 6:31 in practice: decide how you would want to be treated, and put it in writing for everyone.
