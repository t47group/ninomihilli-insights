The worst time to ask a bank for money is when you need it.

When cash is tight, your recent financials look weaker, your balance is low, and you are negotiating under pressure. Lenders can see all of that. The best time to open a line of credit is when the business is healthy and you do not need to draw on it at all.

A line of credit is not a sign of weakness. Used correctly, it is a shock absorber: a way to cover the gap between when you pay your people and when your customers pay you. Here is how I think about getting one and using it well.

This is general guidance, not financial or legal advice. Talk through terms with your banker, CPA, and attorney before you sign.

## What a line of credit is for

A revolving line of credit lets you borrow up to a set limit, repay, and borrow again. You pay interest only on what you draw, though many lines also carry fees.

Good uses:

- **Timing gaps.** Payroll goes out before a large receivable comes in.
- **Seasonal swings.** You build up labor or inventory ahead of a busy season and get paid after it.
- **Growth in working capital.** A new contract requires you to carry more receivables than before.
- **True emergencies.** An unexpected but temporary disruption.

Poor uses:

- Covering ongoing operating losses
- Funding long-term assets like buildings or major equipment, which usually belong in a term loan
- Paying owner distributions the business has not earned

If you are drawing on the line every month and never paying it down to zero, it has stopped being a line of credit and become a loan you did not plan for.

## What lenders typically look at

Each lender has its own criteria, but in my experience the conversation usually centers on:

- **Time in business and track record**
- **Financial statements**, often two to three years plus year-to-date
- **Tax returns**, business and sometimes personal
- **Accounts receivable aging**, especially for lines that are tied to receivables
- **Cash flow coverage**, meaning whether the business generates enough cash to service debt
- **Existing debt** and other obligations
- **Personal credit and guarantees**, since many small business lines require an owner's personal guarantee
- **Collateral**, which may include receivables, inventory, equipment, or a blanket lien on business assets

Clean, timely financials make every part of this easier. If your books are behind, catch them up before you apply.

## How to size it

There is no universal formula, but a practical starting point is your real cash gap.

1. Look at your 13-week cash flow forecast and find the lowest projected point
2. Add your largest realistic receivable delay — a major customer paying 30 days late, for example
3. Consider your seasonal peak, if you have one

The line should comfortably cover a bad-but-plausible stretch, not just an average month. Many lenders also tie the available amount to a percentage of eligible receivables or inventory, called a borrowing base. Ask how yours will be calculated.

## Read the terms that matter

Interest rate gets the attention, but these terms often matter more:

- **Fees:** origination, annual, unused-line, and draw fees
- **Covenants:** financial ratios you must maintain, and what happens if you miss one
- **Reporting requirements:** how often you must send financial statements or borrowing base certificates
- **Renewal:** many lines renew annually, and the lender can change terms or decline to renew
- **Cleanup provisions:** some lines require you to bring the balance to zero for a period each year
- **Personal guarantee:** exactly what you are personally on the hook for
- **Cross-default:** whether a problem on another loan triggers a default here

Have your attorney read the agreement before signing. Covenants and guarantees are where owners get surprised.

## Consider SBA-backed options

If a conventional line is hard to get, ask lenders about SBA-guaranteed programs. The SBA's 7(a) program can be used for short- and long-term working capital, with a maximum 7(a) loan amount of $5 million, and the SBA also runs a 7(a) Working Capital Pilot aimed at businesses that need to fulfill large contracts or borrow against receivables or inventory.

Program details change, and not every lender offers every option. Ask an SBA-participating lender what is currently available and what it would mean for your business.

## Rules for using it well

Once you have a line, discipline matters more than the limit.

1. **Know why you are drawing.** Every draw should tie to a specific timing gap with a specific repayment source.
2. **Pay it down when the cash arrives.** When the receivable lands, the line gets repaid first.
3. **Track it in your forecast.** Draws and repayments belong in your 13-week cash flow.
4. **Watch the trend.** If the average balance creeps up quarter after quarter, find out why.
5. **Do not let it fund losses.** If the line is covering a structural problem, fix the problem.

## Build the banking relationship

A line of credit is easier to get, increase, and renew when your banker knows you. That means:

- Sending financial statements on time, even when not required
- Telling them early when something changes, good or bad
- Meeting at least once a year to talk about your plans
- Asking about an increase before you need it

Bankers respond well to owners who show up with a forecast and a plan. They respond poorly to surprises.

## A quick checklist

- Financials current and reconciled
- 13-week forecast showing your real cash gap
- Target line size based on a plausible bad stretch
- Two or three lenders compared, including fees and covenants
- Attorney review of the agreement and any guarantee
- Written internal rules for when to draw and when to repay
- Annual meeting with your banker on the calendar

## A matter of preparation

Opening a line while you are healthy is simply wise planning. It protects your team's paychecks, your vendors' payments, and your own peace of mind in the weeks when timing does not cooperate. It is the same principle behind every good financial habit: prepare in the calm season so you are not making decisions in a panic.
