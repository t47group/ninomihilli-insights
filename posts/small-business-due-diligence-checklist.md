Buying an existing business can be one of the best ways to grow. It can also be one of the fastest ways to inherit someone else's problems.

The difference is usually due diligence. Not the price, not the financing structure, not how good the seller seemed at dinner. What you verified before closing, and what you assumed.

Most small business buyers rely on a few years of tax returns and the seller's word. That is not enough. Below is a working checklist organized by area. Not every item applies to every deal, but you should be able to explain why you skipped any item that does not.

A note before we start: this is an operator's checklist, not legal or tax advice. Every acquisition should involve a qualified attorney and CPA, and often a quality-of-earnings provider. Use this list to organize your own questions and to make sure your advisors are covering what matters to you as the person who will run the business.

## Financial

- Three to five years of tax returns
- Three to five years of profit and loss statements and balance sheets
- Monthly financials for the last 24 months, to see seasonality and trend
- Reconciliation between tax returns, financial statements, and bank deposits
- Bank statements for at least the last 12 to 24 months
- A list of every owner add-back, with documentation for each
- Accounts receivable aging, and the collection history of the largest balances
- Accounts payable aging, including anything past due
- All debt, liens, leases, and personal guarantees
- Working capital trend: how much cash the business needs to operate normally
- Capital expenditures for the past three years and what is coming due
- Sales tax, payroll tax, and other tax filings current — confirmed by your CPA

**Owner add-backs deserve extra attention.** They are where small business valuations get inflated. A legitimate add-back is a real expense that will not continue under new ownership. A family member on payroll who does real work is not an add-back, because you will have to replace that work.

## Customers and revenue

- Revenue by customer for the last three years
- Concentration: percentage of revenue from the top one, five, and ten customers
- Customer contracts, terms, and whether they can be assigned to a new owner
- Customer churn: who left in the last three years, and why
- Pricing history: when prices last increased, and how customers responded
- Gross margin by customer or service line
- Sales pipeline and recent wins
- Any customer relationships that depend personally on the seller
- Permission, at the appropriate stage, to speak with key customers

If one customer represents a large share of revenue and that relationship runs through the seller, you are not buying a business. You are buying a relationship that may not transfer.

## Employees

- Organization chart and headcount by role
- Compensation, benefits, and tenure for every employee
- Employment agreements, non-competes, and offer letters (have your attorney review enforceability)
- Key employees: who the business cannot run without
- Turnover history over three years
- Worker classification: employees versus contractors, reviewed by your attorney or CPA
- Employee handbook and policies
- Open or past employment claims
- A plan for retaining key people through and after the transition

## Operations

- Documented processes, or how work actually gets done
- Key vendors, contract terms, and alternative suppliers
- Equipment list, condition, and maintenance records
- Inventory count and valuation, where relevant
- Technology stack: software, licenses, data ownership, and who holds the passwords
- Facility lease terms, renewal options, and landlord consent requirements
- Required licenses and permits, and whether they transfer
- Insurance policies and claims history
- Quality, safety, or compliance incidents in the last three years

## Legal and risk

These belong with your attorney. Your job is to make sure they get asked.

- Entity documents and ownership records
- Asset purchase versus stock purchase structure, and what liabilities come with each
- Pending or threatened litigation
- Intellectual property: trademarks, domains, and content ownership
- Environmental or regulatory exposure for the industry
- Representations, warranties, and indemnification in the purchase agreement

## Seller and transition

- Why the seller is selling, in their words, and whether the reason checks out
- What the seller does each week, hour by hour
- Transition period length and the seller's specific responsibilities during it
- Seller financing or earnout terms that keep the seller invested in a good handoff
- Non-compete and non-solicitation terms for the seller, reviewed by counsel

The hour-by-hour week is the item I would not skip. Ask the seller to walk through a typical week. Every task they personally perform is a task you will need to perform or hire for. Owners often underestimate how much of the business lives in their own head.

## How to work through the list

1. **Request documents in one organized list** and track what has been received.
2. **Start with the financial items.** If the numbers do not reconcile, much of the rest may not matter.
3. **Build your own model** of the business under your ownership, including your salary, replacement costs for the seller's work, and debt service.
4. **Flag every gap** between what you were told and what you found.
5. **Decide what each gap is worth.** Some lower the price. Some change the structure. Some end the deal.
6. **Give yourself permission to walk away.** The most expensive deals are the ones buyers were too invested in to stop.

## Diligence as fairness

Good diligence protects both sides. A buyer who understands the business is less likely to end up in a dispute after closing. A seller who answers openly gets a smoother deal and a business that continues to serve the people they built it for. I try to approach every acquisition conversation the way I would want to be approached if I were the one selling.
