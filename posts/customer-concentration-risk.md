Landing a big customer feels like winning. And it is, right up until that customer becomes so big that the business cannot survive without them.

Customer concentration is one of the most common hidden risks I see in growing service companies. It rarely announces itself. One account grows a little faster than the rest, year after year, until one day a purchasing manager's decision in another city determines whether you make payroll.

The goal is not to avoid large customers. Large customers are often how small businesses grow. The goal is to know your exposure, plan for it, and reduce it on purpose.

## How to measure it

Start with a simple table. Pull revenue for the trailing twelve months by customer, sorted from largest to smallest. Then calculate:

- **Top customer share:** the largest customer's revenue as a percentage of total revenue
- **Top five share:** the five largest customers combined, as a percentage of total
- **Gross margin by customer** for the top ten, because revenue concentration and profit concentration are not always the same

Then look at the trend. Run the same numbers for the prior two years. Concentration that is rising is a different problem from concentration that is stable or falling.

There is no single threshold that is safe for every business. Many buyers, lenders, and advisors start asking hard questions once one customer represents a large share of revenue. What matters is that you know the number, track it, and have a view on what level you are comfortable with.

## Why it is more dangerous than it looks

Losing a large customer does not just remove revenue. It hits several things at once.

**Cash.** If the customer leaves, the receivables still have to be collected, and sometimes a departing customer becomes slow to pay.

**Margin.** Large customers often negotiate the lowest prices. You may have more revenue concentrated in the account than profit, which can make the loss less painful than it looks. Or, if the account carries a big share of your fixed costs, it can be far worse.

**People.** In service businesses, teams are often built around a specific customer. Losing the account can mean difficult decisions about people who did nothing wrong.

**Negotiating power.** When a customer knows they are a large share of your business, they have leverage over price, terms, and scope. That leverage tends to get used.

**Value.** If you ever sell, raise capital, or borrow, concentration is one of the first things a buyer or lender examines. Heavy concentration can reduce what someone is willing to pay or lend, or change the terms.

## Run the "what if" before you have to

Every owner with a large customer should model the loss of that customer once a year. Ask:

1. What happens to revenue and gross profit in the first 90 days?
2. Which costs go away immediately, and which remain?
3. What happens to the 13-week cash flow forecast?
4. Which people and roles are affected?
5. What debt covenants or lease obligations could be strained?
6. How long until new business could replace part of the gap?

You do not need to like the answers. You need to know them while you can still act.

## How to reduce concentration

There are only a few real levers, and most of them take time.

**Grow the rest of the book.** The most reliable way to reduce concentration is to add customers, not lose the big one. Set a specific target: for example, a share of new sales capacity dedicated to accounts outside your top customer.

**Broaden within the big account.** If one customer is essential, reduce the risk that a single relationship ends it. Serve multiple locations, departments, or decision-makers. Build relationships beyond your primary contact.

**Lengthen and strengthen the agreement.** A longer contract with reasonable notice periods gives you time to adjust if the customer decides to leave. Have your attorney review termination and notice terms.

**Price for the risk.** If a customer represents an outsized share of your business and pays the lowest rates, the economics may not justify the dependence. Check whether the account clears your margin floor.

**Build a cash reserve proportional to the exposure.** The more concentrated your revenue, the larger your cushion should be.

**Diversify services.** Offering a related service to existing smaller customers can grow the base without the cost of finding entirely new accounts.

## Do not starve the big customer

There is a trap on the other side. Owners worried about concentration sometimes underinvest in their largest account. Service slips, and the customer leaves — creating the very loss the owner feared.

Keep serving your largest customer exceptionally well. Diversification should come from growth elsewhere, not neglect here.

## Put it on the dashboard

Concentration should not be something you calculate once and forget. Add two numbers to your monthly review:

- Top customer share of trailing twelve-month revenue
- Top five share of trailing twelve-month revenue

When either moves meaningfully, discuss why.

## A concentration checklist

- Trailing twelve-month revenue by customer, sorted
- Top one and top five shares calculated and trended over three years
- Gross margin for the top ten accounts
- Annual "loss of top customer" scenario modeled
- Contract notice and termination terms reviewed
- Cash reserve sized to your exposure
- A written goal for growth outside the largest accounts

## Humility in a good season

The time to work on concentration is when the big customer is happy and paying on time. That is when it feels least necessary, which is exactly why most owners wait. Planning for the loss of something good is not pessimism. It is the kind of humility that keeps a business standing for the people who depend on it.
