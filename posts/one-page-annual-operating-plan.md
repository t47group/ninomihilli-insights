Most small businesses do one of two things with annual planning. They skip it entirely, or they produce a long document that nobody opens again after January.

Neither works. The first leaves the team guessing about priorities. The second creates the illusion of a plan without the substance.

What I recommend instead is a single page. One page that answers what the business is trying to accomplish next year, how you will know if it is working, and who owns each piece. It is short enough that every leader can keep it in their head, and specific enough to guide real decisions.

Q4 is the right time to build it. You have most of the year's results in hand, the next year has not started, and there is still time to get your team aligned before January.

## Why one page

Constraint forces clarity. When you only have room for a handful of priorities, you have to choose. Choosing is the point.

A one-page plan is also easier to use. It can be printed, posted, reviewed in the weekly meeting, and referred to when someone asks whether a new opportunity fits. Plans that get used are plans that get followed.

## The sections of the page

Here is the structure I use. Each section is short.

**1. Purpose (one sentence)**

Why the business exists and who it serves. This rarely changes year to year, but it belongs at the top to anchor everything else.

**2. Three-year picture (three to five bullets)**

Where you want the business to be in three years. Revenue range, number of locations or customers, team size, the kind of company you want it to be. This keeps next year's plan connected to something larger.

**3. Next year's targets (four to six numbers)**

The specific results you are committing to for the year. Typically:

- Revenue
- Gross margin
- Net profit or cash generated
- One customer measure (retention, satisfaction, number of active accounts)
- One people measure (headcount, retention, key hires)
- One operational measure that matters most in your business

**4. Priorities (three to five)**

The major initiatives that will move those targets. Each should be specific enough to know whether it happened. "Improve sales" is not a priority. "Hire and onboard two account managers by the end of Q2" is.

**5. Owners and milestones**

For each priority: one owner, and a milestone for each quarter.

**6. What we will not do (two or three items)**

Things you are deliberately choosing not to pursue this year. This section prevents distraction and gives your team permission to say no.

**7. Key risks (two or three items)**

The biggest threats to the plan and how you will watch for them.

## How to build it in five steps

**Step 1: Review this year (week one)**

Before planning next year, take a clear-eyed look at this one. Gather your leadership team and work through:

- What were our targets, and where did we land?
- What worked better than expected?
- What did not work, and why?
- What did we learn about our customers, team, and market?
- What did we start that we should stop?

Write the answers down. Patterns from this review should shape next year's priorities.

**Step 2: Draft targets from the bottom up (week two)**

Ask each leader to estimate what their area can realistically deliver, with the resources they would need. Compare those to your own top-down view. The gap between the two is where the useful conversation happens.

Connect the targets to your financial model. If revenue grows, what does that require in people, cash, and capacity? Your CPA or financial lead should review the plan for tax and cash implications.

**Step 3: Choose priorities (week three)**

List every initiative people want to pursue. It will be too many. Score each by impact on the targets and effort required. Choose three to five. Move the rest to "not this year."

**Step 4: Assign owners and milestones (week three)**

Every priority gets one owner. Not a committee, one person. That person drafts quarterly milestones and the resources they need.

**Step 5: Finalize and share (week four)**

Fit it on one page. Review it with the leadership team one final time. Then share it with the entire company in a way that makes sense for your culture: an all-hands meeting, team meetings, or a printed copy for every location.

## Running the plan through the year

A plan only matters if you use it. The rhythm I recommend:

- **Weekly:** Your KPI review tracks the numbers that feed your annual targets.
- **Monthly:** Leadership reviews progress on each priority. Green, yellow, or red, with a one-line update.
- **Quarterly:** A half-day session to review milestones, adjust priorities if needed, and set the next quarter's milestones.
- **Annually:** Repeat the full process in Q4.

Adjusting the plan mid-year is not a failure. Markets change. The discipline is in making changes deliberately and in the open, not letting priorities quietly drift.

## A Q4 planning checklist

1. Schedule planning sessions with your leadership team before mid-November
2. Complete a written review of this year's results
3. Update your three-year picture
4. Gather bottom-up targets from each leader
5. Build or update your financial model for next year
6. Choose three to five priorities and list what you will not do
7. Assign an owner and quarterly milestones to each priority
8. Identify key risks and early warning signs
9. Fit everything on one page
10. Share the plan with the whole team before January
11. Put monthly and quarterly reviews on the calendar now

## Planning as stewardship

A clear plan is a gift to your team. It tells people what matters, where their work fits, and how success will be measured. It reduces the guesswork that wears people down. And it gives an owner the discipline to say no to good ideas so the best ones get done.
