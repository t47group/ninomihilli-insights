A retirement plan is one of the most valuable things a small business can offer, both for the owner and for the team. It helps you recruit and keep good people, and it can be one of the more useful tax planning tools available to owners.

Yet many small businesses never set one up. The options sound complicated, the paperwork sounds intimidating, and it is easy to keep pushing the decision to next year.

December is a good time to look at this seriously, because some plan deadlines are tied to the end of the year and others to your tax return. Here is a plain-language comparison of the three plans small businesses most often consider.

This is general information, not tax, legal, or investment advice. Retirement plans carry real compliance obligations. Work with your CPA and a qualified plan provider before choosing.

## The three common options

### SEP (Simplified Employee Pension)

A SEP is an employer-funded plan. The business contributes to IRAs for eligible employees, including the owner. Employees do not make their own salary deferrals into a SEP.

Key points, according to the IRS:

- Contributions can be up to 25% of compensation, subject to an annual dollar cap. The 2026 defined contribution limit is $72,000.
- Most SEPs require the business to contribute the same percentage of pay for every eligible employee.
- Eligible employees generally include those age 21 or older who worked for the business in at least three of the last five years and earned at least a minimum amount — $800 for 2026.
- A SEP can be set up for a year as late as the business's tax return due date, including extensions, and contributions are due by that same deadline.

**Best fit:** Owners with few or no employees who want flexibility, since contributions can vary year to year. Less attractive if you have many eligible employees, because you must contribute the same percentage for them as for yourself.

### SIMPLE IRA

A SIMPLE IRA lets employees make salary deferrals, with a required employer contribution.

Key points, according to the IRS:

- Generally available to businesses with 100 or fewer employees that do not maintain another retirement plan.
- The 2026 employee deferral limit is $17,000, with a $4,000 catch-up for those 50 and older ($5,250 for ages 60 to 63). Some SIMPLE plans qualify for a higher limit of $18,100.
- The employer must either match employee deferrals dollar for dollar up to 3% of pay (which can be reduced, but not below 1%, under certain rules) or make a 2% nonelective contribution for every eligible employee.
- A new SIMPLE IRA can generally be set up any time from January 1 through October 1 of the year it takes effect, with an exception for new businesses formed after October 1.

**Best fit:** Small teams that want employees to save for themselves, with lower administrative cost than a 401(k). Note the timing: for most businesses, the October 1 window has passed for 2026, so a SIMPLE IRA now is a 2027 decision.

### 401(k)

A 401(k) offers the most flexibility and the highest potential contribution limits, with more administration.

Key points, according to the IRS:

- The 2026 employee deferral limit is $24,500, with an $8,000 catch-up for those 50 and older and $11,250 for ages 60 to 63.
- Total contributions from employee and employer combined can reach $72,000 for 2026, not counting catch-ups.
- Plan design options include employer matching, profit sharing, and safe harbor designs. Some designs require nondiscrimination testing.
- Many 401(k) plans file an annual Form 5500 and have more ongoing compliance work.

A **one-participant or "solo" 401(k)** covers an owner with no employees other than a spouse. It can allow higher contributions for some owners than a SEP, because the owner can contribute as both employee and employer.

**Timing:** The IRS states that a defined contribution plan can generally be set up by the employer's tax filing due date, including extensions, for the tax year. However, employee salary deferrals generally must be elected by the end of the tax year. The IRS also notes that, beginning with 2023, a sole proprietor with no employees can adopt a 401(k) after the end of the tax year if it is adopted by the filing deadline, not including extensions. If you want deferrals to count for 2026, talk to a provider now.

**Best fit:** Businesses that want employee deferrals with higher limits, or owners who want to maximize their own contributions and can handle the administration.

## The startup credits most owners do not know about

The IRS offers tax credits to help small employers start a plan:

- **Startup cost credit:** For employers with 50 or fewer employees who received at least $5,000 in compensation, the credit can cover 100% of eligible startup costs (50% for 51 to 100 employees), up to a cap that can reach $5,000 per year, for three years.
- **Auto-enrollment credit:** $500 per year for three years for adding an automatic enrollment feature.
- **Employer contribution credit:** For employers with up to 50 employees, a credit for a percentage of employer contributions per employee, up to $1,000 per employee, phasing down over five years. Contributions for higher-paid employees above an IRS threshold do not count.

Eligibility rules apply, including having at least one non-highly compensated employee participating. Ask your CPA whether your business qualifies. For many small employers, the credits significantly reduce the cost of getting started.

## How I would make the decision

1. **List your goals.** Is the priority your own retirement savings, recruiting and retention, tax planning, or all three?
2. **Count eligible employees** and estimate who would participate.
3. **Decide how much you can commit** to employer contributions every year, not just in a strong year.
4. **Compare total cost**, including provider fees, administration, and your time.
5. **Check the deadlines** for the year you want contributions to count.
6. **Ask about startup credits** before you sign anything.
7. **Choose a provider** that explains fees clearly and handles compliance well.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing a plan for the owner's benefit without considering employee costs.** Many plans require contributions for employees too.
- **Missing the setup deadline** for the year you wanted.
- **Not following the plan document.** The written plan controls. Operating differently creates compliance problems.
- **Ignoring fees.** Small percentage fees add up over decades for everyone in the plan.
- **Setting it and forgetting it.** Review the plan annually as the business grows.

## A long-term commitment to your people

A retirement plan says something about how you see your team: not just as people who work for you this year, but as people whose futures matter. It is a practical expression of stewardship, and for many small businesses it is more achievable than owners assume.
