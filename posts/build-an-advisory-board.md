Most owners start looking for advisors in the middle of a crisis. A cash crunch, a partner dispute, an offer to buy the company, a key employee leaving. By then, you are asking strangers to understand your business in a week and help you make the biggest decision of the year.

An advisory board built in a calm season works differently. The members know your business, your numbers, and your goals. When the hard moment comes, they are already up to speed, and they have watched you make decisions long enough to tell you when you are about to make a bad one.

I serve as Vice-Chairperson of the advisory council for the Salvation Army Kroc Center in Phoenix. The setting is different from a private company, but the lesson carries over: a group of committed people with different perspectives sees things no single leader can.

## Advisory board versus board of directors

These are not the same thing, and the difference matters.

A **board of directors** has legal authority and fiduciary duties. It can make binding decisions, including about the CEO.

An **advisory board** has no legal authority. It gives counsel. The owner keeps full control and decides what to do with the advice.

For most privately held small and mid-size businesses, an advisory board is the right starting point. It brings outside perspective without changing ownership or governance. If you are considering a formal board, talk to your attorney about the legal implications first.

## Who to invite

Aim for three to five people. Fewer than three and you lose the benefit of different views. More than five and meetings become hard to run and harder to schedule.

Build around the gaps in your own experience. A useful mix often includes:

- **A finance person** — a CFO, banker, or experienced CPA who reads financial statements quickly and asks hard questions about cash and risk
- **An operator** — someone who has run a business larger than yours, ideally a few stages ahead
- **An industry expert** — someone who knows your market, customers, and competitors
- **A specialist for your next challenge** — sales, technology, acquisitions, or whatever stands between you and your next stage
- **A values anchor** — someone whose judgment and character you trust, who will ask whether a decision is right, not only whether it is profitable

Avoid filling seats with friends who will agree with you, current vendors who have a financial interest in your decisions, or anyone you would be uncomfortable showing your real numbers.

## How to ask

People are more willing to serve than owners expect, especially when the ask is clear. Put the request in writing and include:

1. Why you are forming the board and what you hope to accomplish
2. Why you are asking them specifically
3. The time commitment: number of meetings, length, and preparation
4. The term: one year with an option to renew works well
5. Compensation, if any
6. Confidentiality expectations

A clear term matters. It gives both sides an easy exit if the fit is not right.

## Compensation

Options range from no compensation to a meeting fee to a small equity grant. Many advisory board members of small businesses serve for a modest fee or simply for the relationship and the interest of the work.

Whatever you choose, keep it simple and consistent across members. If you are considering equity, involve your attorney and CPA. Equity has tax and control implications that are easy to get wrong.

At minimum, cover expenses and respect their time. Send materials early, start on time, end on time.

## Structuring the meetings

Quarterly meetings work well for most companies. Each meeting runs two to three hours.

**Before the meeting (one week ahead), send:**

- A one-page summary of the quarter: what went well, what did not
- Key financials and your weekly KPI trend
- Two or three specific questions you want help with

**Meeting agenda:**

1. **Business update (20 minutes).** Brief. They already read the materials.
2. **Deep dive (60–90 minutes).** One or two strategic questions. This is where the value is.
3. **Open floor (20 minutes).** What are they seeing that you are not?
4. **Commitments (10 minutes).** What you will do before the next meeting.

**After the meeting:** Send a short summary of decisions and follow-ups within a few days. At the next meeting, report on what you did with their advice, including when you chose not to follow it and why.

That last part builds trust. Advisors stay engaged when they see their counsel taken seriously.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Only sharing good news.** An advisory board that sees a polished version of your business cannot help with the real one.
- **Making meetings presentations.** If you talk for ninety minutes, you have an audience, not advisors.
- **No preparation.** Advisors who receive materials the night before give shallow advice.
- **Never refreshing the group.** As the business changes, your gaps change. Rotate members as needed.
- **Asking for advice you have already decided against.** If your mind is made up, say so.

## A 60-day launch plan

1. **Week 1:** Write down your three biggest challenges for the next two years.
2. **Week 2:** Identify the expertise gaps behind those challenges.
3. **Weeks 3–4:** Build a list of eight to ten candidates. Ask trusted contacts for introductions.
4. **Weeks 5–6:** Have informal conversations with your top choices.
5. **Week 7:** Send written invitations with clear terms.
6. **Week 8:** Prepare materials and schedule the first meeting.

## Humility as a strategy

An advisory board requires an owner to say, regularly and in front of people they respect, "here is what I do not know." That is uncomfortable. It is also one of the healthiest habits a leader can build. Proverbs says plans fail for lack of counsel, but with many advisers they succeed. I have found that to be true in business as much as anywhere.
